1. Equilibrium

A beam that is not moving is in static equilibrium. For loads in the plane of the beam, two equations must hold:

$$\sum F_y = 0 \qquad\qquad \sum M_{\text{any point}} = 0$$

A pin gives one vertical reaction and a roller gives one, so a simply supported beam has two unknowns. A fixed support gives a vertical force and a moment, which is again two unknowns. Two equations and two unknowns means these beams are statically determinate: the reactions come from statics alone. (Horizontal reactions are zero here because every load is vertical.)

Sign conventions: loads are positive downward, reactions positive upward, and moments positive counterclockwise.

2. Distributed loads → equivalent point loads

For the equilibrium equations, a distributed load \(w(x)\) can be replaced by a single resultant force equal to the area under the load diagram, acting at the area's centroid. A linearly varying (trapezoidal) load splits into a rectangle and a triangle:

$$F_{\text{rect}} = w_1 L_w \;\;\text{at}\;\; \tfrac{1}{2}L_w \qquad F_{\text{tri}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(w_2 - w_1) L_w \;\;\text{at}\;\; \tfrac{2}{3}L_w$$

Here \(L_w\) is the loaded length and the distances are measured from the start of the load.

4. Shear and bending moment (method of sections)

Cut the beam at any position \(x\) and draw a free-body diagram of the piece on the left. The cut face carries an internal shear force \(V\) and bending moment \(M\), which keep that piece in equilibrium:

$$V(x) = \sum_{\text{left of } x} (\text{upward forces}) \qquad M(x) = \sum_{\text{left of } x} F\,(x - x_F) \;-\; \sum_{\text{left of } x} C$$

Positive \(M\) means sagging (the beam bends into a smile). Repeating the cut at every \(x\) gives the diagrams. Three shortcuts help you check them by hand:

  • \(\dfrac{dV}{dx} = -w(x)\): the slope of the shear diagram is the load intensity, so shear is flat with no load, linear under a uniform load, and a parabola under a triangular load.
  • \(\dfrac{dM}{dx} = V(x)\): the slope of the moment diagram is the shear, so M is largest where V crosses zero.
  • A point load makes \(V\) jump by the load. A couple makes \(M\) jump by the couple (clockwise jumps up, counterclockwise jumps down).

5. Stress and strain from bending

This step goes slightly past statics, into the opening topic of mechanics of materials. Once \(M\) is known, the flexure formula gives the normal stress at height \(y\) above the neutral axis (the centroid of the cross section):

$$\sigma = -\frac{M\,y}{I} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_{\max} = \frac{|M|\,c}{I}$$

\(I\) is the cross section's second moment of area (a geometric property, computed like a centroid) and \(c\) is the distance to the farthest fiber. Hooke's law turns stress into strain, the fractional stretch of the material:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E}$$

The average shear stress \(\tau_{avg} = V/A\) is the statics-level estimate of the shear stress. The true distribution through the depth is a mechanics-of-materials topic.

Assumptions: the beam is straight and slender, deflections are small, the material is linear elastic, and the cross section is symmetric about its horizontal axis.
